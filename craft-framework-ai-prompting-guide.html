The CRAFT framework: how to get professional results from free AI tools

By Remi Grant August 3, 2026 9 min read

Most people prompt AI the way they'd type into a search bar — a few words, then frustration when the result comes back generic. A one-line request gets a one-line-quality answer, because the model has nothing specific to work with. Five minutes spent structuring the request almost always beats twenty minutes spent rewriting a bad output.

A small-business owner recently forwarded a cold outreach email he'd asked ChatGPT to draft. It opened with "I hope this message finds you well" and never got more specific than "innovative solution." He'd spent twenty minutes editing it and still didn't like it. The tool wasn't the problem — the five-word prompt he'd given it was.

Why vague prompts produce vague answers

A large language model doesn't know your situation, your audience, or what "good" looks like to you unless you tell it. Ask it to "write a cold email to an investor" and it returns the statistical average of every cold email in its training data — which is exactly as generic as that sounds.

It also helps to know that most tools start each new conversation with no memory of previous ones, unless you're using a feature that specifically carries context across sessions. Treat each new chat as a fresh briefing, not a continuation, unless you've confirmed otherwise.

The four prompt types worth knowing

Nearly every effective prompt is a mix of these four approaches:

  • Zero-shot — plain instructions, no examples: "Write a cold email to an investor."
  • Few-shot — instructions plus examples of the style you want: "Here are two emails I like — write mine in that style."
  • Chain-of-thought — asking the model to reason in steps before answering: "First explain why this investor would care, then draft the email."
  • Persona / role — assigning the AI a specific voice or expertise: "You are a direct-response copywriter."

The framework below combines all four into a single repeatable structure.

The CRAFT framework

CRAFT is five questions, answered in order, before you hit enter.

  • Context — What's the situation? Who are you? "I run a 40-seat Italian restaurant. Three-year relationship with this supplier."
  • Role — Who should the AI become? "You are a warm but firm restaurant owner who values relationships."
  • Ask — What exactly do you need? "Write an email requesting a 10% volume discount, framed as collaborative, not a demand."
  • Format — How should it look? "Five sentences. Friendly opening. No guilt-tripping."
  • Tune — What needs improving after the first draft? "Shorter. Warmer. Cut the third line."

Most weak prompts skip straight to Ask. The Context and Role steps do more work than the other three combined — they're what separates a generic draft from one you'd actually send.

Information without constraints produces volume. Information with constraints produces quality.

CRAFT in action: two real requests, rewritten

A restaurant owner negotiating with a supplier

Before: "Write an email to my food supplier asking for a discount."

After: "I run a 40-seat Italian restaurant. We've ordered from this supplier weekly for three years, roughly $3,000 a week, and never missed a payment. Write an email asking for a 10% volume discount — reference the loyalty without begging, and make it feel collaborative rather than a demand. Five sentences, friendly opening, no guilt-tripping. Show me two versions: one soft, one more direct."

The second prompt gives the model a real relationship to reference, a real number, and clear boundaries on tone — which is exactly what turns a template into something usable.

A founder writing investor outreach

Before: "Write a cold email to an investor for my startup."

After: "I'm the founder of a pre-revenue edtech startup that helps students prep for exams over WhatsApp — 5,000 active users, 40% weekly retention. I'm emailing an investor known for backing affordable-education companies. Write a four-sentence email that shows traction without bragging and makes her curious enough to reply. Don't use 'hope this email finds you well' or 'disrupting.'"

Same tool, same free plan. The only thing that changed was how much the founder was willing to explain before asking for the output.

The mistakes that quietly wreck most prompts

  • The five-word prompt. If it's shorter than a text message to a friend, it's too short — aim to cover Context, Role, and Ask at minimum.
  • Accepting the first draft. The first response is a starting point, not a final answer. "Shorter," "cut the jargon," and "that line is too corporate" are all valid follow-ups.
  • No negative instructions. Telling the model what to avoid — buzzwords, a certain length, a certain tone — is often more useful than telling it what to include.
  • Mixing unrelated topics in one chat. A cold email request followed by a recipe request followed by exam prep pushes useful context out of a limited memory window. Start a new chat per topic.
  • Blaming the tool. Before switching apps, check whether the prompt actually included context, a role, a format, and at least one round of refinement.

Which free tool to reach for

Start with one tool and get comfortable with it before comparing others — switching tools is often a way to avoid improving the prompt itself. As a general starting point, ChatGPT's free tier handles the widest range of everyday writing and brainstorming tasks, Claude tends to handle long documents and nuanced feedback well, and Perplexity is built specifically for research that needs citations attached.

Frequently asked questions

What is the CRAFT framework?

A five-step method — Context, Role, Ask, Format, Tune — for structuring an AI prompt so the model has what it needs to produce a usable answer on the first or second try.

Why do I keep getting generic AI responses?

Generic prompts produce generic answers. Add who you are, who the output is for, what tone you want, and what to avoid.

Do I need a paid AI plan to get good results?

Not to start. Prompt structure closes most of the quality gap — it's worth mastering on a free plan before assuming an upgrade is what's missing.
